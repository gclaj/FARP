The problem reach

The deep fight is farther than a full tank.

An attack helicopter's combat radius is finite. Fuel, ordnance, a reserve and time over the target all draw on the same tank. Push the target past that radius and the deep attack becomes a one-way trip, or it does not launch at all.

Doctrine what a FARP is

A temporary facility that gives aircraft fuel and ammunition close to the fight.

That is the doctrinal definition, from Army aviation's field manual and the techniques publication for forward arming and refueling points. The point is distance: every mile the fuel moves forward is a mile the aircraft does not have to fly back. Doctrine describes four variants, each trading permanence for speed.

Active

Manned and running

Equipment set, crews in place, fuel flowing. The standard FARP supporting a continuous operation.

Silent

Set, then waits

Established and camouflaged but not operating. It is activated only when aircraft are inbound, to stay hidden until needed.

Jump

Small, fast, air-delivered

A minimal footprint FARP that displaces rapidly, often with the fuel arriving on the aircraft themselves. Built to support one deep push, then move or close.

Rolling

Moves with the fight

Displaces by bounds behind the maneuvering unit so support keeps pace with the advance.

How the fuel gets there. In this concept a CH-47 carries Extended Range Fuel System tanks and pumps internally and lands at the site. The Chinook itself is the fuel point. The unit's terms for that configuration are FATCOW and WETCOW.

What makes it a seizure. The site is not ours yet. An infantry company air assaults in, isolates the ground, takes it, and holds it long enough for the fuel to arrive, the attack aircraft to turn, and everyone to leave.

Why it matters the Division deep area

A FARP moves the start line.

The Division's deep area is the ground beyond what its brigades are fighting over, where aviation shapes the enemy before the main effort arrives. Reaching it is a fuel problem before it is a tactics problem.

  • Reach. Refuel 200 miles forward and targets 300 miles out are inside the radius.
  • Time on station. Aircraft arrive over the target with a full tank instead of a reserve.
  • Tempo. Turn aircraft forward in minutes rather than flying hours back to rearm.
  • Mass. More sorties on the objective in the same window. The mission statement says it plainly: mass combat power in the Division deep area.

For the 101st this is the enabler for the large-scale, long-range air assault. The deep attack sets conditions on the far objective. The jump FARP sets conditions for the deep attack.

Doctrine how an air assault is planned

Plan it backward from the objective.

Airborne and air assault doctrine builds five plans in reverse order. Each one constrains the next. For a FARP seizure the ground tactical plan starts with a question most air assaults never ask: can a Chinook full of fuel land here?

Planning events

IPC · AMCM · AMB

Initial planning conference, air mission coordination meeting, air mission brief. The aviation unit and the ground unit build one plan, not two.

Pathfinders

Make the zones real

Select, mark and control landing and pickup zones, give inbound aircraft terminal guidance, and run the pickup zone when it is time to leave.

Rehearsal

Cold loads and leader certs

Loading drills with the actual aircraft, leader certification, and rehearsals are how the company buys down the risk of operating 180 miles from help.

FM 3-99 · reverse planning sequence07
Phasing overview

Four phases, three hours on the ground.

The CONOP names four phases by what the force is doing. The Storm Surge timeline adds the clock: about two and a half hours of flight, an hour to seize, an hour to fuel, an hour to get out. The defense phase is not a block of time. It begins the moment the first aircraft lands and ends when the last one leaves.

That compression is the design. A fuel point is loud, lit and predictable, so the concept keeps the window short. Everything in the following slides is about making each hour fit.

Phase I condition setting

Pick the ground the Chinook can land on, then plan everything else.

Key tasks: air assault planning and FARP site selection. The site has to satisfy three customers at once.

  • The aircraft. Size and surface for a CH-47 with fuel aboard, approach and departure headings clear of trees, slope and dust acceptable at night.
  • The target. Close enough that attack aircraft arrive with real station time, far enough that the fuel point is not on the objective.
  • The defense. Few avenues of approach, cover for the perimeter, and a second site to jump to. Storm Surge plans three WETCOW sites across five locations.

Phase II air assault and seizure

Isolate. Seize. Mark the ground.

Key tasks: ground task force air assault (Lift 1), FARP seizure, pathfinder operations. Night landing, land navigation, seize.

  1. H-hour. Wheels down. Serials land on three zones on their briefed headings, weapons free, trees the hazard.
  2. Isolate. Team Isolate moves from LZ Vulture and LZ Falcon to blocking positions on the avenues into the site.
  3. Seize. Team Seize moves from LZ Owl and takes OBJ Bernese, the ground the fuel point needs.
  4. Mark. The security squad and pathfinders establish PZ Cedar: lights, headings, a control point. The seizure is reported no later than H+1:00.

Phase III FARP operations

The Chinook is the gas station.

Key tasks: FARP task force air assault (Lift 2), FARP establishment, deep attack air assault (Lift 3). Pathfinders run the zone.

  1. H+1:00. Lift 2 lands. The fuel Chinook and the task force Chinook arrive on the marked zone with the fuel handlers, engineers, mortars and scouts.
  2. Establish. Hoses out to two refuel points. Echo company's handlers from 6-101 run the points; the security squad holds the zone.
  3. Lift 3 lands. Attack aircraft arrive on the points, fuel and arm with rotors turning.
  4. Turn forward. They depart for the target with a full tank. FARP operations complete by H+2:00.

Phase IV FARP defense

Loud, lit and predictable. So keep it short and keep them off it.

Key task: defend the FARP. The defense is not a perimeter around the fuel. It is the isolation Team Isolate built in Phase II, held for as long as the window is open.

  • Block the avenues. Three blocking positions on the likely approaches, tied to the obstacles the sappers emplace.
  • See first. Scout observation posts beyond the blocking positions. Fire support observers with the mortars.
  • Respond fast. The mortar section is the company's own indirect fire. Attack aircraft on the points are a last resort, not the plan.
  • Talk home. Primary, alternate, contingency and emergency: encrypted messaging over tactical satellite, then the team awareness kit, then a satellite voice net, then cell or high frequency.

The jump trigger. If the site is compromised, or the second fuel site is better placed for the next turn, the FARP displaces. That is the difference between a jump FARP and an active one: it is planned to move before it has to.

Phase IV runs from first landing to last departure12
Collapse H+2:00 to H+3:00

The site goes dark. The attack is already deep.

Storm Surge's fifth phase, reconsolidation, is what makes this a jump FARP rather than a base. The fuel point exists for one turn of the attack and then it is gone.

  • H+2:00. FARP operations complete. Hoses in, points closed, the Chinook with the tanks lifts first.
  • Pickup zone. Pathfinders control the pickup: chalks staged, aircraft inbound on 090, staggered left, weapons tight.
  • Collapse the isolation. Blocking positions fold in last, in reverse order, under the mortars until the mortars themselves lift.
  • H+3:00. Reconsolidated at home station, with full accountability of people and equipment as the friendly end state.

Risk and what buys it down

A company, alone, 180 miles out.

Risk to force

Distance from help

A company (minus) with limited sustainment, 180 nautical miles from the home airfield. Casualty evacuation and resupply are both aircraft that have to be planned, not assumed.

Risk to mission

A lit fuel point

Rotors turning, fuel flowing, aircraft on the ground on a schedule. The window is the vulnerability. The concept answers with isolation and a short clock.

Training risk

New pathfinders

The company accepts the risk of operating with newly qualified pathfinders, because a Division pathfinder company is who this mission would be given to.

Mitigation

Carry the day

One day of supply of rations issued before wheels up and one more carried per soldier on the aircraft.

Mitigation

Rehearse the whole thing

Deliberate rehearsals, air assault planning with the aviation battalion, cold loads on the real aircraft, and leader certification before execution.

Mitigation

Prove it first

Validate the task force on a training objective at full distance before the concept is used to underwrite a deep attack.

Proof of concept Operation Storm Surge

Fly it at full distance before it counts.

Purpose. Validate a FARP seizure mission set that would be given to a Division pathfinder company, and prove the company's internal loading, command and control, enabler and FARP packages in an unfamiliar pickup zone.

  • 185 NM with no refuel, using the extended range fuel system on the first serial.
  • WETCOW extends attack aviation 60 NM beyond the objective.
  • Three WETCOWs across five FARP locations, dispersed and secured at once, supporting more than one aircraft type.
  • Hippo concept at 370 NM total, with sustainment and command nodes delivered by air, including an air-deliverable tactical command post.

End state: the task force validated, every soldier and piece of equipment accounted for, nothing damaged on the ground. Then an after action review on 30 October and the concept goes back into the Division's planning with numbers behind it.
