The problem reach

The deep fight is farther than a full tank.

An attack helicopter's combat radius is finite. Fuel, ordnance, a reserve and time over the target all draw on the same tank. Push the target past that radius and the deep attack becomes a one-way trip, or it does not launch at all.

Why a forward fuel point exists02
Doctrine what a FARP is

A temporary facility that gives aircraft fuel and ammunition close to the fight.

That is the doctrinal definition, from Army aviation's field manual and the techniques publication for forward arming and refueling points. Every mile the fuel moves forward is a mile the aircraft does not have to fly back. Doctrine describes four variants.

Active

Manned and running

Equipment set, crews in place, fuel flowing. The standard FARP behind a continuous operation.

Silent

Set, then waits

Established and camouflaged but not operating. Activated only when aircraft are inbound.

Jump

Small, fast, air-delivered

Minimal footprint, displaces rapidly, often with the fuel arriving on the aircraft themselves. Built for one deep push.

Rolling

Moves with the fight

Displaces by bounds behind the maneuvering unit so support keeps pace with the advance.

Why it matters the Division deep area

A FARP moves the start line.

The Division's deep area is the ground beyond what its brigades are fighting over, where aviation shapes the enemy before the main effort arrives. Reaching it is a fuel problem before it is a tactics problem.

  • Reach. Refuel 200 miles forward and targets 300 miles out are inside the radius.
  • Time on station. Arrive with a full tank instead of a reserve.
  • Tempo. Turn aircraft forward in minutes instead of flying hours back.
  • Mass. More sorties on the objective in the same window. The mission statement says it: mass combat power in the Division deep area.

For the 101st this is the enabler for the large-scale, long-range air assault. The deep attack sets conditions on the far objective. The jump FARP sets conditions for the deep attack.

Phase IV · FARP defense runs the whole time the site is open

The CONOP names four phases by what the force is doing. The Storm Surge timeline adds the clock: about two and a half hours of flight, an hour to seize, an hour to fuel, an hour to get out. The defense phase is not a block of time. It begins when the first aircraft lands and ends when the last one leaves.

That compression is the design. A fuel point is loud, lit and predictable, so the concept keeps the window short. Everything that follows is about making each hour fit.

CONOP phasing · Storm Surge H-hour sequence08
Phase I condition setting

Pick the ground the Chinook can land on, then plan everything else.

Key tasks: air assault planning and FARP site selection. The site has to satisfy three customers at once.

  • The aircraft. Size and surface for a CH-47 with fuel aboard, approach and departure headings clear of trees, slope and dust acceptable at night.
  • The target. Close enough for real station time, far enough that the fuel point is not on the objective.
  • The defense. Few avenues of approach, cover for the perimeter, and a second site to jump to. Storm Surge plans three WETCOW sites across five locations.

Phase IV FARP defense

Loud, lit and predictable. Keep it short, keep them off it.

Key task: defend the FARP. The defense is not a perimeter around the fuel. It is the three objectives seized in Phase II, held for as long as the window is open.

  • Hold the objectives. Husky covers north, Poodle and Bernese cover east along the road. Sectors from the three apexes, tied to the sappers' obstacles.
  • See first. Scout observation posts beyond the blocking positions. Fire support observers with the mortars.
  • Respond fast. The mortar section is the company's own indirect fire. Aircraft on the points are a last resort, not the plan.
  • Talk home. Primary, alternate, contingency, emergency: encrypted messaging over tactical satellite, then the team awareness kit, then a satellite voice net, then cell or HF.

The jump trigger. If the site is compromised, or the second fuel site is better placed for the next turn, the FARP displaces. A jump FARP is planned to move before it has to.

Phase IV runs from first landing to last departure12
Security why the objectives are where they are

Three objectives make a patrol base around the fuel.

Draw a line from Husky to Poodle to Bernese and back. PZ Cedar sits inside it. Every sector faces out from an apex, and the thing being protected sits in the middle with the command post. Infantry already know this shape.

  • Apexes carry the weight. In a patrol base the machine guns sit at the apexes to cover the likely approaches. Here each objective holds an avenue: Husky north, Poodle and Bernese the road from the east.
  • Sides are the squads. The legs between objectives are covered by the squads on them and by the scouts' observation posts, so nothing walks in between.
  • Centre is the headquarters. A patrol base keeps the command post and aid station in the middle. Here the middle holds PZ Cedar, the rapid refuel point and the NOMAD.
  • Same drill, bigger triangle. The company is running a patrol base at objective scale: occupy, secure, work from the centre, then collapse it in reverse order.

Collapse H+2:00 to H+3:00

The site goes dark. The attack is already deep.

Storm Surge's fifth phase, reconsolidation, is what makes this a jump FARP rather than a base. The fuel point exists for one turn of the attack and then it is gone.

  • H+2:00. FARP operations complete. Hoses in, points closed, the Chinook with the tanks lifts first.
  • Pickup zone. Pathfinders control the pickup: chalks staged, aircraft inbound on 090, staggered left, weapons tight.
  • Collapse the isolation. Blocking positions fold in last, in reverse order, under the mortars until the mortars themselves lift.
  • H+3:00. Reconsolidated at home station, with full accountability of people and equipment as the friendly end state.

Risk and what buys it down

A company, alone, 180 miles out.

Risk to force

Distance from help

A company (minus) with limited sustainment, 180 nautical miles from the home airfield. Casualty evacuation and resupply are both aircraft that have to be planned, not assumed.

Risk to mission

A lit fuel point

Rotors turning, fuel flowing, aircraft on the ground on a schedule. The window is the vulnerability. The concept answers with isolation and a short clock.

Training risk

New pathfinders

The company accepts the risk of operating with newly qualified pathfinders, because a Division pathfinder company is who this mission would be given to.

Mitigation

Carry the day

One day of supply of rations issued before wheels up and one more carried per soldier on the aircraft.

Mitigation

Rehearse the whole thing

Deliberate rehearsals, planning with the aviation battalion, cold loads on the real aircraft, leader certification before execution.

Mitigation

Prove it first

Validate the task force on a training objective at full distance before the concept underwrites a deep attack.

Proof of concept Operation Storm Surge

Fly it at full distance before it counts.

Purpose. Validate a FARP seizure mission set that would be given to a Division pathfinder company, and prove the company's loading, command and control, enabler and FARP packages in an unfamiliar pickup zone.

  • 185 NM with no refuel, using the extended range fuel system on the first serial.
  • WETCOW extends attack aviation 60 NM beyond the objective.
  • Three WETCOWs across five FARP locations, dispersed and secured at once, supporting more than one aircraft type.
  • Hippo concept at 370 NM total, with sustainment and command nodes delivered by air, including an air-deliverable tactical command post.
